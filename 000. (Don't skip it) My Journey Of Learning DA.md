# 4. Detailed Understanding of the Roles

## 4.1 Collecting Data

A Data Analyst needs to obtain the appropriate data required to answer a particular business or analytical question.

Data may come from sources such as:

- SQL databases
- Data warehouses
- Excel or CSV files
- APIs
- Business applications
- CRM systems
- ERP systems
- Website or application analytics
- Surveys
- Public datasets
- Internal company reports

The important point is that data collection is not simply about gathering as much data as possible. An analyst needs to determine **what data is relevant to the question being investigated**.

### Example

If an e-commerce company asks:

> "Why did our sales decrease last month?"

The analyst may need information about:

- Orders
- Products
- Customers
- Prices
- Discounts
- Dates
- Locations
- Website traffic
- Marketing campaigns

The analyst can then obtain the relevant data from the organization's available sources.

---

## 4.2 Processing and Cleaning Data

Raw data is often incomplete, inconsistent, duplicated, or incorrectly formatted.

Therefore, Data Analysts need to inspect, clean, and prepare data before performing meaningful analysis.

Common problems include:

- Missing values
- Duplicate records
- Incorrect data types
- Invalid dates
- Inconsistent categories
- Incorrectly entered values
- Outliers
- Formatting inconsistencies
- Data from different sources that do not match

### Example

A dataset might contain:

| Customer | Age | City | Purchase |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Rahim | 25 | Dhaka | 500 |
| Karim | NULL | Dhaka | 700 |
| Rahim | 25 | Dhaka | 500 |
| Hasan | 250 | Chittagong | 900 |
| Rafi | 30 | DHAKA | 600 |

The analyst would need to investigate issues such as:

- The missing age.
- The duplicate Rahim record.
- The suspicious age value of 250.
- The inconsistent representation of Dhaka.

The goal is to produce data that is **reliable and suitable for analysis**.

---

## 4.3 Analyzing Data

After the data has been prepared, the Data Analyst examines it to answer relevant questions and identify patterns, relationships, trends, and differences.

Typical analytical activities include:

- Filtering data
- Sorting data
- Grouping data
- Aggregating data
- Calculating statistics
- Comparing groups
- Identifying trends
- Segmenting data
- Examining relationships between variables
- Testing hypotheses when appropriate

### Example

If monthly sales are:

| Month | Sales |
|---|---:|
| January | $100,000 |
| February | $105,000 |
| March | $110,000 |
| April | $108,000 |
| May | $82,000 |

The analyst may investigate:

- When did the decline begin?
- Which products contributed to the decline?
- Which regions were affected?
- Did website traffic change?
- Did conversion rates change?
- Did prices or discounts change?
- Were there changes in marketing campaigns?

The purpose is not merely to calculate numbers, but to **understand what the data indicates about the business question**.

---

## 4.4 Visualizing and Reporting

Data Analysts need to communicate their findings clearly.

Instead of presenting large amounts of raw data, analysts can use appropriate visualizations and reports to make important information easier to understand.

Common outputs include:

- Charts
- Graphs
- Tables
- Reports
- Dashboards
- Interactive visualizations

Common tools include:

- Microsoft Excel
- Power BI
- Tableau
- Python
- Matplotlib
- Seaborn

### Example

Instead of giving a manager a table containing several months of sales figures, an analyst could create a line chart showing the sales trend.

A good visualization should have a clear analytical purpose. Visualization is not simply about making a report look attractive; it should help the audience understand the relevant information.

---

## 4.5 Making Recommendations

Data Analysis can be used to support business decisions.

After identifying important findings, an analyst may communicate possible actions or areas that require further investigation.

### Example

Suppose an analysis shows:

- Product A's sales decreased significantly.
- Website traffic remained relatively stable.
- The decline was concentrated in a particular region.
- A competitor introduced a major promotion during the same period.

An analyst could report these findings and recommend that the relevant business team investigate factors such as pricing, promotions, product availability, or competitive activity.

A Data Analyst does not necessarily make the final business decision. Instead, the analyst provides **evidence, insights, and analysis that can support decision-making**.

---

## 4.6 Using Powerful Tools

Tools allow Data Analysts to perform their work efficiently.

Different tools are useful for different tasks.

### Excel / Google Sheets

Useful for:

- Spreadsheet analysis
- Formulas
- Data cleaning
- Pivot tables
- Basic charts
- Quick exploratory analysis

### SQL

Used to retrieve and analyze data stored in relational databases.

Example:

```sql
SELECT
    product,
    SUM(sales) AS total_sales
FROM orders
GROUP BY product
ORDER BY total_sales DESC;
```

## 4.7 Working with Teams

Data Analysts rarely work completely alone. They often work with people from different departments to understand business problems, clarify requirements, obtain data, communicate findings, and support decision-making.

Depending on the organization, a Data Analyst may work with:

- Managers
- Marketing teams
- Sales teams
- Finance teams
- Product teams
- Data Engineers
- Analytics Engineers
- Software Engineers
- Business stakeholders
- Clients

### Why Teamwork Is Important

A Data Analyst may have strong technical skills, but the analysis is only useful if it addresses the **right business question**.

For example, a manager might say:

> "Our sales are bad. Can you analyze them?"

This request is too broad. The Data Analyst may need to communicate with the manager and clarify:

- What does "bad sales" mean?
- Are we talking about revenue, profit, number of orders, or conversion rate?
- Which period should be analyzed?
- Should the results be compared with the previous month or previous year?
- Which products or regions should be investigated?
- What business decision will the analysis support?

After understanding the requirement, the analyst can determine what data is needed and what type of analysis should be performed.

### Working with Technical Teams

Data Analysts may also collaborate with technical teams.

For example:

**Data Engineer → Data Analyst**

A Data Engineer may help make the required data available from databases or data warehouses.

**Data Analyst → Business Team**

The Data Analyst may communicate findings and insights to managers or business teams.

**Data Analyst ↔ Product Team**

A Product Analyst may work with product managers to analyze user behavior, engagement, retention, or other product-related metrics.

### Example

Suppose an e-commerce company notices that its sales have decreased.

The Data Analyst might:

1. Discuss the problem with the **Sales Team**.
2. Obtain relevant data with help from the **Data Engineering Team**.
3. Analyze sales, customers, products, and regions.
4. Create a dashboard using **Power BI**.
5. Present the findings to **Management**.
6. Discuss possible explanations with the **Marketing Team**.
7. Provide data-based insights that can support future decisions.

Therefore, teamwork is not separate from Data Analysis. It can be involved throughout the entire analytical process.

### Key Learning

A Data Analyst needs more than technical skills. They also need to be able to:

- Understand stakeholder requirements
- Ask relevant questions
- Communicate clearly
- Explain analytical findings
- Work with people from different departments
- Present information to both technical and non-technical audiences
- Collaborate effectively with technical teams

In practice, **communication and teamwork are important because Data Analysis exists to solve real-world problems, not simply to produce numbers, charts, or dashboards.**
